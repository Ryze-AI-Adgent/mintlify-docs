Generative engine optimization (GEO) is the practice of making a website more likely to be retrieved and cited by AI systems that generate answers rather than return a list of links — ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Claude, and Gemini.

## Also called AEO and LLM SEO

**Answer engine optimization (AEO)** and **LLM SEO** describe overlapping practices, and the terms are often used interchangeably. The useful distinction:

| | focus | surface |
|---|---|---|
| **AEO** | being *the answer* to a specific question | featured snippets, voice, AI assistants |
| **GEO** | being *a cited source* inside a generated answer | ChatGPT, Perplexity, AI Overviews |
| **LLM SEO** | how content is parsed and reproduced by the model | any AI answer |

AEO predates generative engines — it grew out of optimizing for featured snippets. In practice the techniques converge: all three reward direct, factual, well-structured content, so this page covers them together.

## How it differs from SEO

Classic SEO optimizes for **ranking** in a list of results. GEO optimizes for **inclusion** in a generated answer.

The practical differences:

- **The unit of success is a citation, not a position.** There is no rank 1. A page is either drawn into the answer or it is not.
- **Retrieval happens per-query, at answer time.** Most answer engines run a live search before responding, so the pages they cite are chosen fresh each time rather than from a fixed index position.
- **Results are volatile.** The same question asked twice can produce different sources. GEO is measured as a rate across many queries over time, not by a single result.
- **Passage-level clarity matters more than page-level authority.** A model extracts a specific claim. Pages that state facts plainly and early are easier to extract from than pages that build to a conclusion.

## How to write for AI answers

- **Answer in the first sentence.** State the answer or the fact, then explain. A fact in sentence one survives extraction; a fact in paragraph six may not be reached.
- **One question per page or per section.** A heading that matches the question, followed immediately by the answer.
- **Self-contained passages.** Retrieval often works on sections, not whole pages, so each section should make sense lifted out of the page.
- **Be unambiguous about the subject.** Repeat the product or concept name rather than relying on "it" across paragraphs.
- **State constraints and limits.** Models reproduce caveats when they are stated plainly, which improves answer accuracy about your product.
- **Keep URLs stable.** A moved page loses whatever retrieval history it accumulated.
- **Structured data** where the content type supports it — `Product`, `Organization`, `BreadcrumbList`, `Article`. Google stopped showing FAQ rich results in May 2026 and HowTo rich results in 2023, so `FAQPage` and `HowTo` markup no longer earns a result on Google; the question-and-answer content itself still matters for AI answers. See [structured data for AI search](/ai-visibility/structured-data-for-ai).

## What tends to get cited

In our experience, these tend to get cited:

- Pages that define **one specific thing** and say what it is in the first sentence
- Documentation and help-centre content, which is factual and stable
- Pages that state real numbers — limits, intervals, prices, thresholds
- Pages that sit alongside the primary specification they implement

Marketing pages, listicles, and "ultimate guides" are cited comparatively rarely. Persuasive writing gives a model little to extract.

## Limits

GEO influences **retrieval** — which pages a model finds and quotes when it searches. When a model answers from memory without searching, GEO has no effect. Mentions come from the model's training data and move slowly; citations come from live retrieval and respond to content — see [AI visibility](/concepts/ai-visibility).
