Benchmarks are the most misused numbers in ecommerce. This page is mostly about why, and what to use instead.

## Why external benchmarks mislead

Published ecommerce conversion averages typically land somewhere in the **1–3%** range, and that figure is close to useless for any individual store.

Conversion rate varies enormously by:

- **Category** — a £15 consumable and a £2,000 sofa are not comparable
- **Price point** — higher prices convert lower and should
- **Traffic source** — branded search converts several times better than cold paid social
- **Device** — mobile converts lower than desktop, consistently
- **New versus returning** — returning visitors convert far higher
- **Market and season**

A store at 1.2% might be excellent (high-value considered purchase, cold traffic) or poor (low-price impulse category, branded traffic). The benchmark cannot tell you which.

<Note>
Treat any single "good conversion rate" figure with suspicion, including the range above. It is a distribution, not a target.
</Note>

## Use your own baseline

The only reliable comparison is your store against itself.

Record, before making changes:

| baseline | why |
|---|---|
| Conversion rate **by device** | Mobile and desktop must be tracked separately |
| Conversion rate **by traffic source** | Blended rates hide everything useful |
| **Revenue per visitor** | The metric that actually matters |
| **Average order value** | So you notice a conversion gain that cost you AOV |
| **Funnel step completion** | Where loss occurs, not just that it does |
| **Add-to-cart rate** | Separates product page problems from checkout problems |

Then measure change against those, over comparable periods, ideally year over year.

## Segment or learn nothing

A blended sitewide conversion rate conceals almost everything actionable. The useful cuts:

- Mobile vs desktop
- Paid vs organic vs direct vs email
- New vs returning
- By landing page template
- By category

A sitewide rate that is flat can hide mobile falling while desktop rises.

## Rate of change beats absolute level

The useful question is not "is 1.8% good?" but "is it better than last quarter, on comparable traffic, and why?"

Improving from 1.4% to 1.8% is a 29% revenue increase on the same traffic. Whether 1.8% beats an industry average is irrelevant to that.
